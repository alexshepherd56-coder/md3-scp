## Case

Ron Camper, aged 21 years presents complaining of a painful 2cm nodule in the periumbilical region. He advises that he has had a nodule that has "come and gone" since birth but it has never been painful. He now feels nauseous.

## Questions

<CaseQuestion
  number={1}
  question="Outline your assessment and management of Ron."
>

**Assessment Priorities:**
        

          - Need to determine if this is an **urgent situation** - is there evidence of **strangulation**? Is the hernia **reducible**?

          - Is the nausea related to the lump, or is there another cause?

          - **Examination of the lump** - location, size, reducibility, tenderness, cough impulse

          - Investigation of this patient will be determined by the urgency of the situation

        

        

**Key Concern:** Given Ron's nausea and new-onset pain in a previously asymptomatic hernia, there is concern for **incarceration or strangulation** which would require urgent surgical intervention.

</CaseQuestion>

<CaseQuestion
  number={2}
  question="What are the 3 common types of umbilical hernia and what is the pathophysiology of each type?"
>

<h3>1. Congenital (Infantile) Umbilical Hernia</h3>
        

**Pathophysiology:**
        

This condition is caused by a **widening of the natural defect in the linea alba at birth**.
        

          - The complex folding of the developing embryo results in the formation of the coelomic cavity

          - The apices of the folds form the **umbilical ring** through which the midgut communicates with the yolk sac and the connecting stalk (containing umbilical vein and arteries) passes to the placenta

          - The developing gut grows more rapidly than the coelomic capacity and so **prolapses into the wide base of the umbilical cord** - the extra-embryonic coelom - as a temporary hernia

          - The abdominal cavity subsequently enlarges, and by the **12th week** the herniated intestinal loops return, undergoing rotation as they do so

          - After the return of the gut, the umbilical ring constricts, but a defect persists until birth for passage of umbilical vessels

          - After delivery, the umbilical vessels thrombose, and fibrous tissue forms and condenses to plug the defect

          - A **congenital umbilical hernia results if this sequence is incomplete**

        

        <h3>2. True Umbilical Hernia</h3>
        

**Pathophysiology:**
        

          - Protrusion **through the umbilical scar**, everting the umbilicus, whose attenuated fibres are at the apex of the hernial sac

          - The cause is often secondary to an **increase in the volume of contents of the abdominal cavity**:
            

              - Obesity

              - Ascites

              - Large benign or malignant intra-abdominal tumours

            

          

        

        <h3>3. Para-umbilical Hernia</h3>
        

**Pathophysiology:**
        

          - The **weakest area of the umbilical scar is at the superior aspect** between the umbilical vein and the upper margin of the umbilical ring

          - It is at this point that a para-umbilical hernia develops

          - The emerging sac **displaces the umbilical scar**, which lies below and slightly to one side

          - These hernias are **more common than true umbilical hernias** and typically found in obese middle-aged patients

          - The **neck of the hernia is often narrow** - in consequence, tissues that enter have great difficulty leaving

          - Adhesions form and the hernia becomes **irreducible**

          - The sac progressively acquires more contents and may become very large

          - Contents are usually **omentum**, often with small bowel or transverse colon

          - Frequently the sac becomes **loculated** when adhesions form between omentum and peritoneum

          - **At risk of strangulation**

</CaseQuestion>

<CaseQuestion
  number={3}
  question="How does the common type of umbilical hernia typically present?"
>

**Clinical Presentation of Para-umbilical Hernia (most common type):**

        

**Symptoms:**
        

          - **Local pain and swelling at the navel**

          - Non-specific gastrointestinal symptoms are common

          - Features of recurrent intestinal obstruction may have occurred

        

        

**Physical Examination:**
        

          - The **umbilicus assumes a crescent shape**

          - Inspection and palpation reveal a swelling **just above the umbilicus** whose centre (in contrast to true umbilical hernia) is **not attached to the apex of the protrusion**

          - In **grossly obese patients**, the swelling may not be obvious to the naked eye and moreover is barely palpable

          - In others the hernia may be **enormous**

          - Usually it is **reducible (at least in part)** and there is a **cough impulse**

          - If reduction is possible, the palpable defect can be of any size, from **one fingertip to admitting the fist**

</CaseQuestion>

<CaseQuestion
  number={4}
  question="Summarise the treatment of umbilical hernias."
>

<h3>True Umbilical Hernia</h3>
        

          - Any **underlying cause should be sought and dealt with**

          - In the rare event that nothing is found and the hernia is causing symptoms, it is treated as a para-umbilical hernia

        

        <h3>Para-umbilical Hernia</h3>
        

**Indications for Surgery:**
        

          - Symptomatic hernias require treatment

          - There is a **high risk of strangulation**, and repair should be advised, **even in the absence of symptoms**

        

        

**Surgical Technique:**
        

          - The usual procedure is to:
            

              - Mobilise the sac and its contents

              - Return the contents to the abdomen

              - Close the neck

              - Repair the abdominal wall by overlapping its layers

            

          

          - In herniae **less than 5mm**: fascial closure

          - Others consider: **onlay or sublay mesh, plug mesh, sandwich mesh if recurrent**

        

        <h3>Strangulated Umbilical Hernia</h3>
        

**Diagnostic Trap:**
        

          - The patient with **severe abdominal pain and vomiting** and a **soft non-tender umbilical hernia** is a diagnostic trap

          - The loculated nature of the hernia allows a strangulated portion of bowel to go unnoticed clinically

          - In other instances, the local features of strangulation may be obvious

        

        

**Operative Approach:**
        

          - Same as for elective case

          - Strangulating contents dealt with according to their state

          - Devitalized tissue resected if necessary

</CaseQuestion>

<CaseQuestion

  question="Briefly describe the cutaneous nerve supply of the abdominal wall."
>

**Cutaneous Nerve Supply of the Abdominal Wall:**

        

The anterior rami of **thoracic spinal nerves T7 to T12** follow the inferior slope of the lateral parts of the ribs and cross the costal margin to enter the abdominal wall.

        

          - **Intercostal nerves T7 to T11** supply skin and muscle of the abdominal wall

          - **Subcostal nerve T12** also supplies skin and muscle of the abdominal wall

          - In addition, **T5 and T6** supply upper parts of the external oblique muscle of the abdominal wall

          - **T6** also supplies cutaneous innervation to skin over the xiphoid

        

        

**Dermatomal Landmarks:**
        

          - T6 - Xiphoid process level

          - T7 - Upper abdomen

          - T8 - Mid-abdomen

          - T10 - Umbilicus level

          - T11 - Lower abdomen

          - T12 - Suprapubic region

          - L1 - Inguinal region

</CaseQuestion>

<CaseQuestion
  number={6}
  question="Briefly describe the anatomy and pathophysiology of incisional hernias."
>

**Definition:**
        

An **incisional hernia** is one that occurs through the wound of a previous operation. It has the same features as a hernia caused by non-surgical injury to the abdominal wall.
        

**Partial dehiscence** of all or part of the deeper fascial layers occurs, but the skin remains intact or eventually heals.

        <h3>Preoperative Factors</h3>
        

          - **Age** - the tissues of the elderly do not heal as well as those of the young

          - **Malnutrition** - protein-calorie malnutrition, vitamin deficiency (vitamin C essential for collagen maturation), trace metal deficiency (zinc required for epithelialisation)

          - **Sepsis** - worsens malnutrition and delays anabolism

          - **Uraemia** - inhibits fibroblast division

          - **Jaundice** - impedes collagen maturation

          - **Obesity** - predisposes to wound infection, seroma and haematoma

          - **Diabetes mellitus** - predisposes to wound infection

          - **Steroids** - have a generalised proteolytic effect

          - **Peritoneal contamination (peritonitis)** - predisposes to wound infection

        

        <h3>Operative Factors</h3>
        

          - **Type of incision** - vertical incisions more prone to hernia than transverse ones

          - **Technique and materials** - tension in closure impedes blood supply to wound; badly tied knots can work loose; closure with rapidly absorbable suture material fails to support abdominal wall for sufficient time

          - **Type of operation** - operations involving bowel or urinary tract more likely to develop wound infection

          - **Drains** - a drain passing through the wound often results in a hernia

        

        <h3>Postoperative Factors</h3>
        

          - **Wound infection** - equal in importance with wrong choice of suture material: enzymatic destruction of healing tissues; inflammatory swelling raises tissue tension and impedes blood supply; **5-20% of wound infections result in a hernia**

          - **Abdominal distension** - postoperative ileus increases tension on wound; stitches may cut out

          - **Coughing** - generates wound tension

        

        <h3>Pathological Features</h3>
        

          - Most incisional hernias develop **within 1 year** of an operation

          - It is unusual for a previously sound closure to become herniated **after 3 years**

          - Once a hernia has formed, mechanical forces ensure that it **inexorably enlarges**

          - Incisional hernias are extremely variable - may be wide or narrow-necked

          - Often, as contents accumulate, **adhesions develop** in the sac and just deep to the neck, so the hernia becomes both **irreducible and loculated**

          - **Incarceration and strangulation** then become real dangers

          - The sac can assume huge proportions, eventually housing much of the normal intraperitoneal contents

</CaseQuestion>

<CaseQuestion
  number={7}
  question="Briefly describe the treatment options of incisional hernias."
>

**Indications for Repair:**
        

          - Even **small symptomatic hernias should be repaired early**

          - In asymptomatic hernias the risks of **intestinal obstruction, strangulation and skin ulceration** are such that repair, even in older patients, is also recommended

          - Protracted observation simply allows the hernia to increase in size, and subsequent repair is rendered more difficult and hazardous

        

        

**Surgical Technique:**
        

          - The surgical technique is **the same as for para-umbilical hernias**:
            

              - Mobilise the sac and its contents

              - Return contents to abdomen

              - Close the neck

              - Repair abdominal wall by overlapping its layers

            

          

          - **Larger hernias** may require **prosthetic mesh reconstruction** of the abdominal wall

</CaseQuestion>
